How the numbers were derived

The shape of the comparison is the signal; the exact figures will vary by project. Here is what is in the numbers, and what is not.

Note 01

The V1 figures

NeoArchitect time was calculated from actual file timestamps: first output file on April 16 at 16:08, last app revision on April 17 at approximately 11:22. Roughly twenty hours of wall-clock time across two calendar days, estimated at ~15 hours of focused work after accounting for sleep, breaks, and context switches. The traditional team figure of 7 weeks assumes a parallel-working specialist team — PM, designer, architects, three engineers, QA, writer, release engineer — with typical coordination overhead.

Note 02

The V2 figures

V2 is defined as a scalable version of the product — real backend, multi-tenant data, OAuth/SSO authentication, deployment pipeline, monitoring. The greenfield estimate (10 weeks) reflects a team running full discovery and design from a blank slate. The inheriting estimate (5 weeks) assumes the V1 artifact has answered the product questions, freeing the team to focus on the scalability work itself — which is the bulk of real V2 effort.

Note 03

What is not in these numbers

Coordination overhead — standups, reviews, approvals, cross-team unblocks — typically adds 15–25% to traditional team estimates and is already baked into the 7- and 10-week figures. NeoArchitect figures have no such overhead: one person, one session, one collaborator. Comparing labor cost rather than calendar time makes the gap larger still: ~15 hours versus ~1,200 person-hours for V1 is roughly an 80× compression in human-hours.

Note 04

The docs shipped too

Most traditional builds ship with skeleton documentation at best — a bare README, a half-filled wiki page. What shipped here on day one: a sixty-kilobyte team-facing guide covering architecture, security model, auth patterns, and workflows; a versioned prompt configuration with change rationale; a packaged skill bundle with reusable snippets; and a migration path for legacy data. Documentation was not a cleanup phase. It was part of the artifact.

— What the chart shows, and what it requires —

The chart shows the compression. The compression requires a NeoArchitect. Cut the judgment out of the loop and you keep the volume — but lose the quality, and lose the path that produced the quality in the first place.

P.S. — The chart itself

Because someone will ask.

The traditional production pipeline for an infographic like this would involve a data analyst pulling timestamps, an information designer sketching layouts, a visual designer executing in Figma or Illustrator, a copywriter polishing the stat cards and the caveats nobody reads, a stakeholder review or two, and a front-end developer rebuilding the whole thing as responsive SVG for the web. Call it three to five person-days of real labor — one to two calendar weeks once the review cycles finish their tour of everyone's inbox.

Building it here — with the prior context of the project already loaded, the design language already established by the manifesto, and the timing data already at hand from our own file timestamps — took about twelve minutes, plus a single round of feedback from the stakeholder ("the labels are too close to the bars") and a two-minute nudge of the x-axis to fix it.

A chart about compression, compressed. Recursion, for free.
